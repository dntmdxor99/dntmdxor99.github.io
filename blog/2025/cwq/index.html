Why Weight-only Quantization?

Recent advances in large-scale language models (LLMs), , , have achieved remarkable performance across a wide range of natural language understanding and generation tasks. However, deploying these models in resource‐constrained environments remains a significant challenge. In particular, Transformer‐based LLMs such as Llama-2-70B-hf require approximately 140 GB of high‐precision (FP16) parameter storage, placing them beyond the memory budgets of most edge devices and many data‐center inference clusters. Moreover, the limited bandwidth for transferring weight data between memory and compute units during inference creates a memory-bound bottleneck, restricting overall throughput.

Weight-only quantization—where only the model parameters are stored with reduced bit-width, while activations and arithmetic operations remain in high precision—has emerged as a promising solution. By representing weights in 4-bit or 2-bit instead of FP16, the model size can be reduced by 4× to 8×, which also lowers the memory-traffic overhead during inference. Existing studies show that 4-bit quantization of Llama-2-70B-hf requires only 35 GB of memory (compared to 140 GB for FP16), and 2-bit reduces this further to 17.5 GB. In terms of speed, quantizing a LLM to low-bit has been shown to improve token throughput by mitigating memory bottlenecks and cache misses.

Despite these gains, existing weight-only quantization methods, such as AWQ, GPTQ, and OWQ, remain fundamentally discrete: the precision of model must be chosen from a small set of integer bit-widths (e.g., 2-, 3-, or 4-bits). Such discrete allocation can be overly coarse, leading to suboptimal trade-offs between model size, accuracy, and throughput. Furthermore, state-of-the-art post-training quantization (PTQ) techniques, such as GPTQ and AWQ, depend on calibration datasets and perform layer-dependent optimization: quantization errors propagate through the network, making it difficult to independently assess and adjust each layer’s sensitivity. As shown in Table 1, selecting discrete bit-widths results in highly limited memory footprints and inference performance on GPUs with fixed KV-cache budgets. For example, 4-bit quantization on an A100 (80 GB device memory, 30 GB KV cache) leaves only 15 GB free, whereas 2-bit on an RTX 4090 (24 GB, 5 GB KV cache) leaves merely 1.5 GB—despite identical quantized model sizes—highlighting the clear opportunity to improve performance by leveraging additional memory.

Why Continous Relaxation?

This study focuses on transforming conventional discrete weight-only quantization into a continuous domain. Our motivation stems from the observation that each projection (i.e., linear layer) in an LLM exhibits varying sensitivity to quantization. Leveraging this, we propose a new approach that dynamically assigns different bit-widths to different linear layers, thus enabling the model’s average bit-width to become a continuous value rather than a fixed, discrete one.

However, this approach presents two major challenges. First, most post-training quantization (PTQ) techniques, rely on calibration datasets and are inherently interdependent across layers. As quantization errors propagate from one layer to the next, it becomes difficult to independently assess the sensitivity of each linear layer. Second, there is no clear criterion for assigning bit-widths across layers. While assigning two distinct bit-widths (e.g., 2- and 4-bit) can be managed with a well-defined target average, assigning three or more (e.g., 2-, 3-, and 4-bit) demands a more fine-grained strategy. These limitations necessitate extensive search or hand-crafted tuning, even when bit allocation can be performed in a relatively short time.

To overcome these challenges, we propose a new method that enables optimal bit allocation based on a loss-based criterion within a single forward pass. First, we define a quantization proxy sensitivity metric that eliminates the need to fully quantize the model to identify the sensitivity of each layer, while still effectively representing the layer-wise quantization sensitivity. Second, we utilize the proxy sensitivities for 2-, 3-, and 4-bit quantization to construct a bit-performance Pareto frontier, from which bit combinations suitable for various deployment constraints can be derived. Building on these ideas, we introduce CWQ: Continuous Relaxation for Weight-only Quantization, a novel and computationally efficient method that bridges the gap between discrete and continuous weight-only quantization for large-scale language models.

Data-Dependent and Data-Independent Method

Quantization algorithms for large language models can be classified into two main categories, each offering different trade-offs in terms of accuracy, computational overhead, and flexibility:

Methods like GPTQ and AWQ establish per-layer quantization by analyzing both weights and activations using a calibration dataset. While this activation-aware approach achieves state-of-the-art model quality, generating a new bit-width configuration quantized model necessitates a complete end-to-end quantization process—resulting in significant format-conversion costs, multiple forward passes, and inter-layer dependencies that render comprehensive search infeasible.

Techniques such as Half-Quadratic Quantization (HQQ) perform weight quantization based exclusively on their weight distribution, without utilizing activations or calibration data. Through decoupling the quantization of each layer, these methods remove quantization overhead and enable immediate “assembly” of mixed-precision models as illustrated in Figure 2.

Quantization Sensitivity

A core observation in weight-only quantization is that different linear layers in a Transformer exhibit markedly different tolerance to low-bit representations. To systematically quantify this quantization sensitivity, we adopt the following:

We begin with a fully 4-bit quantized model, using HQQ for weight-only quantization as our data-independent proxy. For each linear layer L_i in turn, we temporarily reduce its precision to 2 bits while keeping all other layers at 4 bits. This “one-at-a-time” drop tests the isolated impact of each layer’s quantization on overall model quality. Rather than performing a full, data-dependent quantization pass for each configuration, we rapidly assemble the mixed-precision model by selecting from pre-quantized 2-, 3-, and 4-bit replicas of each layer. This avoids hours of per-configuration format conversion and calibration. Finally, We measure degradation by the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) between the quantized model’s output distribution and that of the original FP16 model, evaluated on a small calibration set. Specifically,

$$ \text{for logits }z\ (\text{quantized})\ \text{and }\hat z\ (\text{original}), $$ $$ \mathrm{JSD}(z,\hat z) = \tfrac12\bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(s\Vert m) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat s\Vert m)\bigr], \quad s = \mathrm{softmax}(z),\;\hat s = \mathrm{softmax}(\hat z),\;m=\tfrac12(s+\hat s). $$
Figure 2. Illustration of data-dependent vs. data-independent quantization and quantization proxy.
Figure 3. JSD Loss degradation when each linear layer of Llama-2-7B is individually quantized to 2 bits (others at 4 bits). Sensitivity varies widely, motivating selective high-precision allocation.

As Figure 3 demonstrates, certain layers (e.g., value and down projections) dramatically increase loss under 2-bit, whereas others show minimal impact. This JSD Loss validates our strategy that allocate high-precision to high-sensitivity layers.

Loss-based Dynamic Bit Allocation

To optimally distribute a fixed average bit-width budget across layers, we exploit the layer-wise quantization sensitivity measured via Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD).

Denote the set of sensitive layers by $\mathcal{S}$. CWQ achieves the target bit-width by assigning 4-bit to all $i\in\mathcal{S}$ and 2-bit to every other layer. This simple binary assignment matches target bit-width in closed form, eliminating the need for expensive search.

To further reduce the quantization error, CWQ+ allows a third precision level (3-bit). We perform a lightweight grid search over the number k of non-sensitive layers to promote from 2- to 3-bit and sesntivie layer to degrade from 4- to 3-bit, evaluating each candidate via the quantization proxy and selecting the k that minimizes aggregate JSD while exactly satisfying target bit-width. Since the proxy assembly is O(1) per layer, this incurs only O(n) overhead for n layers. With bit-widths allocated per layer, we apply a state-of-the-art, data-dependent quantization method (e.g., AWQ) to each layer at its assigned precision. This last step leverages activation-aware clipping to further reduce quantization error without altering the allocation scheme.

AWQ at 2-bit collapses in Llama-2-7B and Llama-2-13B, whereas CWQ (2.1-bit) outperforms AWQ and enhances zero-shot average from 30.7% to 52.1%. Furthermore, CWQ+ overwhelmingly outperforms AWQ and CWQ, preserving 80.67% of the performance of FP16 at extremely low-bits, demonstrating the effectiveness of CWQ as a technique.

CWQ also performs well with 3-bit, which is interger, which shows that our bit allocation technique is more effective than the traditional single bit allocation technique. In particular, we show the novelty of our adaptive allocation method by considering the sensitivity rather than the traditional AWQ which allocates 3-bit to every layer.

4-bit CWQ is identical to AWQ, so we do not compare it. However, CWQ+ outperforms CWQ, which only uses 2-bit and 4-bit, demonstrating that properly allocating bits can improve performance.

These results demonstrate that loss-based dynamic allocation (CWQ/CWQ+) delivers the greatest gains under stringent memory constraints, while standard PTQ methods are not able to achieve reasonable performance.

Our experimental results demonstrate that CWQ and CWQ+ maintain high model quality across the entire quantization spectrum, with particularly impressive gains at very low bit-widths. Even under extreme compression (≈2 bits), our methods preserve competitive zero-shot accuracy and reasonable perplexity, while at moderate and high precisions they match or exceed the best data-dependent baselines. This consistent robustness across “Fits everywhere” suggests that our loss-based dynamic allocation framework can transform weight-only quantization from a memory-limited optimization into a general-purpose technique for both edge devices and large-scale inference clusters.

Conclusion

Traditional weight-only quantization methods are inherently constrained by discrete bit-width choices (e.g., 2, 3, or 4 bits), leading to coarse trade-offs and underutilized device memory budgets. In this work, we have presented CWQ, the first continuous-relaxation approach that treats each layer’s bit-width as a real-valued variable, allowing fractional average precision and fine-grained control. Building on this framework, we introduced CWQ+, which incorporates a lightweight layer-wise precision search over an additional 3-bit option to further improve the memory-accuracy trade-off.

Experimental results across multiple LLM scales demonstrate that our methods consistently outperform state-of-the-art discrete post-training quantization under tight memory constraints, while matching or exceeding their quality at low to high precisions. The “Fits everywhere” robustness of CWQ and CWQ+ unlocks weight-only quantization for a far broader spectrum of deployment scenarios, from highly memory-limited edge devices to large-scale inference clusters, without sacrificing responsiveness or accuracy.

Limitation

Our algorithm is quite naive, and there is a possibility that there is a better algorithm. Also, we only used group size 128, but we think it is possible to use per-channel or group size 64 to control the bit and memory more fine-grained.
